Most fried electronics abroad aren't bad luck — they're one of a handful of
avoidable mistakes, repeated by travelers every day. Learn to spot these five and
you'll almost never destroy a device.

## 1. Assuming an adapter changes the voltage

The single most expensive misunderstanding in travel. A **plug adapter changes the
shape of your plug only** — it does nothing to the electricity. The voltage flows
straight through, untouched. If you're counting on an adapter to "handle" a foreign
voltage, you aren't protected at all. We break down exactly why in
[adapter vs. converter](/guides/adapter-vs-converter).

## 2. Plugging a single-voltage device into the wrong band

The world runs on two voltage bands:

| Band | Voltage | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 100–127 V | US, Canada, Japan, Mexico |
| High | 220–240 V | Europe, UK, most of Asia, Africa, Australia |

A **dual-voltage** device (labeled "100–240V") handles both automatically. A
**single-voltage** device does not. Send a 120 V-only appliance into a 230 V socket
and you can destroy it the instant you switch it on. Always know which band your
device wants and which band you're traveling to.

## 3. Using an underpowered converter on a heating appliance

If you do use a converter, it has to be rated for your device's wattage. Heating
appliances — hair dryers, straighteners, travel irons — pull enormous power (often
1,500 W). A cheap travel "converter" rated for a handful of watts will overheat,
trip, or burn out under that load. For hot tools, a dual-voltage model is nearly
always the smarter buy; see
[traveling with a hair dryer or straightener](/guides/travel-with-hair-dryer-straightener).

## 4. Trusting a cheap ungrounded adapter with a grounded appliance

Notice the third pin on some plugs? That's the **ground** — a safety path that
protects you if a fault occurs. Many bargain-bin adapters quietly drop it, forcing
a three-pin grounded plug into a two-pin connection. For a laptop charger it may
not matter; for anything metal-bodied or high-power, that missing ground is a real
safety risk. Buy an adapter that preserves the ground pin. More on this in
[grounded vs. ungrounded plugs](/guides/grounded-vs-ungrounded-plugs).

## 5. Not reading the label

Nearly every mistake above is prevented by ten seconds of reading. Every device
prints its input rating on the plug, the charger brick, or a sticker on the base.
Look for a line like:

> INPUT: 100–240V, 50/60Hz

Two numbers with a dash means dual-voltage — adapter only. A single number means
you have to think about voltage bands. The label is the answer key; use it before
you pack, not after something dies.

## Pre-trip safety checklist

- [ ] Read the input label on every device you're bringing.
- [ ] Sorted which are dual-voltage (100–240V) and which are single-voltage.
- [ ] Checked your destination's voltage band and plug type.
- [ ] Packed a quality plug adapter that keeps the ground pin.
- [ ] Left single-voltage heating appliances at home (or bought dual-voltage travel versions).
- [ ] Power banks and spare batteries packed in your carry-on, never checked.

Do this once and the whole trip's electronics take care of themselves. When you
want a definitive answer for a specific country pairing, [run your route](/) — it
returns the plug and voltage verdict in one line.
