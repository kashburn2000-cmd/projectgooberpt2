"Dual voltage" is the most useful phrase in travel electronics. A device that's
dual-voltage can run on any country's mains without a converter — so learning to
spot it on a label instantly answers the biggest question of the trip: *will this
survive over there?*

## What "dual voltage" means

The world's mains supply comes in two bands: a low band of 100–127&nbsp;V and a
high band of 220–240&nbsp;V. A **single-voltage** device is built for just one of
them. A **dual-voltage** device accepts the entire range, from the bottom of the
low band to the top of the high band, and adjusts automatically.

On a label, that full range is written as:

`INPUT: 100–240V ~ 50/60Hz`

Those two numbers with a dash — `100–240V` — are the whole story. They mean the
device handles any mains voltage on Earth. Because it manages the electricity
itself, you never need a voltage converter for it; a plug adapter for the socket
**shape** is all that's ever required.

## Where to find the label

The rating is printed on the device or its power supply, usually in tiny type. Look:

- On the **charger brick** or wall plug, molded or printed into the body
- On the **base or underside** of an appliance
- Near the **DC connector** on a laptop's power adapter
- On a **sticker inside the battery compartment** of some cameras and small gadgets

## How to read it: input is what matters

The one trap is confusing input with output. A charger lists both:

| Label line | What it describes | Do you read it? |
|---|---|---|
| `INPUT: 100–240V` | the mains side, from the wall | **Yes** — this is the voltage that matters |
| `OUTPUT: 5V 3A` | the low DC voltage sent to your device | No — ignore it for travel |

Always base your decision on the **INPUT** figure.

### Label symbols worth knowing

- The `~` symbol means alternating current (AC) — normal mains power. It's not a
  voltage.
- `50/60Hz` covers both mains frequencies, so it's a good sign of a travel-ready
  device.
- A range shown as `100V–240V` or `100-240V` means the same thing, however it's
  punctuated.

## Nearly all your chargers already qualify

Here's the reassuring reality: almost everything you charge is already
dual-voltage. Phone and tablet chargers, laptop bricks, camera and drone chargers,
e-readers, headphones, and power banks are dual-voltage as a rule. If it charges
over USB, the only thing that matters is the little plug or brick feeding the USB
cable — and those are dual-voltage too. Our guide to
[charging USB devices abroad](/guides/charging-usb-devices-abroad) covers this in
detail.

## Single-voltage vs dual-voltage at a glance

The devices that catch people out are the ones with heat or simple motors:

- **Usually dual-voltage:** phone, laptop, and tablet chargers; cameras; most
  electric shavers and toothbrush bases
- **Often single-voltage:** hair dryers, straighteners, curling irons, cheap
  kettles, clothing steamers, and budget appliances

When in doubt, the label — not the category — is the final word.

## Quick label-reading checklist

1. Find the `INPUT` line on the device or its charger.
2. See a range like `100–240V`? It's dual-voltage — pack a plug adapter and you're
   done.
3. See a single number like `120V`? It's single-voltage — crossing voltage bands
   needs a converter or a dual-voltage replacement.
4. Still unsure? [Run your route](/) or read
   [do I need a voltage converter?](/guides/do-i-need-a-voltage-converter).

Then match the plug shape for your destination on our [plug-types page](/plug-types),
and you've covered everything that matters.
