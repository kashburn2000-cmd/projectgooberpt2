If you cross several borders on one trip, a **universal travel adapter** is one
of the most useful things you can pack. But the name oversells it a little — and
misunderstanding what it does is how people fry their electronics. Here's the
straight version.

## What a universal travel adapter actually is

A universal adapter combines the world's most common plug shapes into a single
body. Most cover four types that, between them, reach the majority of countries:

- **Type A** — North America and Japan
- **Type C** — the ungrounded "Europlug" used across most of Europe
- **Type G** — the United Kingdom and Ireland
- **Type I** — Australia, New Zealand, and China

You select the pins you need with a slider or by swapping a module, so one gadget
replaces a drawer full of single-country adapters. To see exactly which shape a
country uses, check our [plug-type reference](/plug-types).

## It is still only an adapter

Here's the caveat that matters most: a universal adapter changes the **shape** of
your plug and nothing else. It does not touch the voltage. Plug a single-voltage
120&nbsp;V appliance into a 230&nbsp;V socket through one of these and you'll
destroy it just as thoroughly as you would with any other adapter.

That's fine for the gear most people carry, because phone chargers, laptop bricks,
and camera chargers are almost all **dual-voltage** — they handle any country's
electricity by themselves. If that term is new to you, read
[dual-voltage explained](/guides/dual-voltage-explained) before you pack.

## The grounding catch

Many universal adapters are **two-pin and ungrounded**, even when the face looks
like a grounded Type G or Type I. Sometimes the earth pin is just molded plastic
that fits the socket without connecting to anything.

For double-insulated electronics — a phone charger or a laptop — that's no
problem. But if you're plugging in something with a metal body that relies on an
earth connection for safety, an ungrounded adapter quietly removes that
protection. Our guide to [grounded vs ungrounded plugs](/guides/grounded-vs-ungrounded-plugs)
explains when to care.

## USB and USB-C ports

Most modern universal adapters add a couple of USB-A ports, and often a USB-C
port. This is genuinely useful — you can charge a phone and a watch straight from
the adapter and leave the AC socket free. Two things to check first:

- **Total wattage.** Built-in ports are usually modest. A USB-C port that tops out
  near 18–20&nbsp;W will charge a phone happily but won't run a power-hungry laptop
  at full speed.
- **They're still just charging.** The ports don't change any of the voltage rules
  above; they're simply a convenient low-voltage DC output.

## Who should buy one — and who shouldn't

The decision usually comes down to how many different sockets your trips involve.

### Buy a universal adapter if you

- Visit several countries with different sockets on a single trip
- Travel often and don't want to track which adapter matches where
- Want built-in USB ports so you can carry fewer chargers

### Stick with a single-country adapter if you

- Only ever travel to one region — it's smaller, cheaper, and often grounded
- Need a guaranteed earth connection for a specific metal-bodied appliance
