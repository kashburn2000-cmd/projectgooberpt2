Voltage converters are bulky, heavy, and — for most modern travellers —
completely unnecessary. But for the wrong device, skipping one is an expensive
mistake. Here's the 30-second check.

## Step 1: Find the label

Every electrical device has its input rating printed on it — on the plug, the
charger brick, or a sticker on the base. Look for a line like `INPUT` or `100–240V`.

## Step 2: Read the voltage range

- **Two numbers with a dash (100–240V):** dual-voltage. It adapts to any country's
  electricity by itself. **You do not need a converter — ever.** Skip to
  [checking your plug type](/plug-types) instead.
- **One number (e.g. 120V, or 230V):** single-voltage. This device only accepts
  one band. Read on.

## Step 3: Compare voltage bands

The world uses two bands:

| Band | Voltage | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 100–127 V | US, Canada, Japan, Mexico, much of South America |
| High | 220–240 V | Europe, UK, Africa, most of Asia, Australia |

If your single-voltage device and your destination are in the **same band**, you're
fine — you may still need a plug adapter, but no converter. If they're in
**different bands**, you need a converter (or a dual-voltage replacement).

## What actually needs a converter?

In practice, the culprits are always the same handful of items:

- Hair dryers, straighteners, and curling irons (unless labelled dual-voltage or "travel")
- Some electric shavers and toothbrush bases
- Cheap kettles, clothing steamers, and small kitchen gadgets
- Older or budget appliances of any kind

And what almost never needs one: **anything with a USB cable or a chunky charger
brick.** Phones, laptops, tablets, cameras, e-readers, headphones — all dual-voltage.

## A word on wattage

If you do buy a converter, match it to your device's wattage. High-draw heating
devices (a 1,500&nbsp;W hair dryer) need a heavy step-down *transformer*, not a
cheap travel "converter" rated for a few watts. Honestly, for heating appliances
it's usually cheaper and lighter to buy a dual-voltage travel model.
