Look closely at the plugs in your bag and you'll notice some have two pins and
some have three. That third pin is the earth, or ground — and whether it's there,
and whether your travel adapter actually carries it through, is a genuine safety
question. Here's what it does and when it matters.

## What the ground pin actually does

Inside a mains appliance, a single loose or damaged wire can touch the metal body
of the device. Without a ground, that casing becomes live, and the next person to
touch it becomes the path to earth — a serious shock. The ground pin prevents
this. It connects the metal casing to earth through a low-resistance wire, so
fault current rushes harmlessly to ground instead of through you, and the sudden
surge trips the fuse or breaker. In short, the earth pin is there for the one bad
day when something inside fails.

## Which plug types are grounded?

Grounding is built into some plug standards and absent from others:

- **Grounded (three contacts):** Types B, D, E, F, G, I, J, K, L, M, N.
- **Ungrounded (two pins):** Types A and C.

[Type C](/plug-types/c), the two-pin Europlug, is the most widely used plug in the
world precisely because it's small and ungrounded — it's meant for low-power,
double-insulated devices. Its grounded continental cousin is
[Type F](/plug-types/f), the Schuko. You can browse them all on the
[plug types](/plug-types) page.

## When grounding matters — and when it doesn't

You don't need an earth connection for every device. Broadly, there are two camps:

| Device | Ground needed? |
|---|---|
| Metal-cased appliances — kettles, irons, some kitchen gear, power tools | Yes; this is exactly what earth protects against |
| Three-pin laptop or monitor power bricks | Yes, if it shipped with a ground pin |
| Double-insulated devices (marked with a square-inside-a-square symbol) | No; no exposed metal to become live |
| Most phone chargers and two-pin USB bricks | No; double-insulated by design |

### The rule of thumb

If your device came with a three-pin plug, its maker decided it needs the earth —
so don't defeat that on the road. If it came with two pins, it was built to be
safe without one.

## The travel-adapter catch

Here's the trap that surprises people. Many cheap travel adapters are only two-pin
on the output side, even when your appliance's plug has three. Push a grounded plug
into one of these and it may physically fit — but the earth pin connects to
nothing. Your grounded appliance silently loses its protection, and you'd never
know until something failed.

If you're carrying anything with a three-pin plug, use a travel adapter that
genuinely passes ground through (it will have the earth contact on both sides).
This is separate from voltage entirely: grounding protects you from faults, while
voltage is about whether your device can handle the local electricity at all.

## Safety checklist

- If a device has a three-pin plug, use a grounded (three-contact) travel adapter —
  never a two-pin one.
- Two-pin, double-insulated devices are fine with a simple two-pin adapter.
- Never file down, snap off, or tape over an earth pin to force a fit.
- Inspect adapters and cables for cracks, bent pins, or heat damage before use.
- When in doubt about a metal-cased appliance, don't improvise — replace it or
  leave it home.
