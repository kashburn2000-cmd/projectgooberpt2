Alongside two voltage bands, the world also runs on two mains **frequencies**:
50&nbsp;Hz and 60&nbsp;Hz. It sounds like one more thing to worry about, but for the
gear most travelers carry it's the least important detail on the label. Here's why
— and the handful of cases where it genuinely counts.

## What mains frequency is

Alternating current doesn't flow in one direction; it reverses back and forth many
times a second. Frequency, measured in hertz (Hz), is simply how many times per
second it does that. At 60&nbsp;Hz the current changes direction 60 times a second;
at 50&nbsp;Hz, 50 times.

That's the whole idea. It's a property of the electricity coming out of the wall,
set by the local grid, and you can't change it with an adapter.

## Who uses which

The split roughly follows the Americas versus the rest of the world:

- **60&nbsp;Hz:** the United States, Canada, Mexico, and most of the Americas, plus
  a few others such as South Korea and Saudi Arabia
- **50&nbsp;Hz:** most of Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia, and New Zealand

### The odd one out: Japan

[Japan](/country/japan) is split down the middle. Eastern Japan, including Tokyo,
runs at 50&nbsp;Hz, while western Japan, including Osaka and Kyoto, runs at
60&nbsp;Hz — a legacy of buying generators from different countries in the 1890s. It
rarely affects visitors, precisely because the electronics you carry don't care
about frequency.

## Why your phone and laptop don't care

Nearly everything you travel with uses a **switch-mode power supply** — the
technology inside every phone charger, laptop brick, and camera charger. The first
thing it does is convert incoming AC to DC, which makes the original frequency
irrelevant almost immediately.

That's why the same charger works identically in Tokyo and Osaka, or in London and
New York. If a device's label reads `50/60Hz`, the manufacturer is telling you
outright that frequency makes no difference to it. This covers essentially all
modern electronics — phones, laptops, tablets, cameras, e-readers, and anything
charged over USB.

## When frequency actually matters

Frequency matters only for devices whose job is tied to the rhythm of the AC
itself — chiefly older gear with **mains-synchronous motors** or parts that spin in
step with the supply:

- **Plug-in clocks and timers** that count mains cycles. A clock built for
  60&nbsp;Hz runs slow on 50&nbsp;Hz, losing roughly ten minutes every hour.
- **Turntables and some older audio gear**, where platter speed is set by the mains
  — a 60&nbsp;Hz turntable spins slightly slow on 50&nbsp;Hz, dropping the pitch.
- **Simple AC motors** in some fans, mechanical timers, and cheap appliances, which
  run about 17% slower when moved from 60&nbsp;Hz to 50&nbsp;Hz, and faster the
  other way.

Even here, most of these devices still run — they just keep time or speed
imperfectly. And none of it applies to the electronics you actually rely on.

## The practical takeaway

For phones, laptops, tablets, cameras, and chargers, ignore frequency completely.
It's already handled. The only time to pause is if you're traveling with an old
plug-in clock, a turntable, or a corded appliance with a simple motor — and even
then the usual concern is voltage, not frequency.
