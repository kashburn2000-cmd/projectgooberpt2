Plug shapes get all the attention, but the difference that can actually wreck your
electronics is **voltage**. The world's mains supply comes in two broad bands, and
knowing which is which — and why — takes the mystery out of traveling with gear.

## Two bands, one planet

Every wall socket delivers electricity at a nominal voltage. Across the world
these cluster into two ranges:

- **Low band — 100–127&nbsp;V:** the United States, Canada, Mexico, Japan, and much
  of South America
- **High band — 220–240&nbsp;V:** most of Europe, the UK, Africa, most of Asia, and
  Australia

The [United States](/country/united-states) runs at about 120&nbsp;V; Germany and
most of Europe at 230&nbsp;V. Japan sits at the very bottom of the low band at
100&nbsp;V.

## A quick history of the split

The divide is an accident of timing.

### The American head start

When Thomas Edison built the first electrical networks in the United States in the
1880s, his incandescent lamps worked well at around 110&nbsp;V, and that became the
American standard. A vast amount of equipment was built around it, so the country
never had a strong reason to change — the figure has only crept up to today's
120&nbsp;V.

### Europe optimizes later

Europe electrified later, and engineers there recognized that a higher voltage
carries the same power with less current. Less current means thinner, cheaper
copper wiring and lower losses, so much of the continent standardized on
220&nbsp;V, later harmonized to 230&nbsp;V. Britain, once on 240&nbsp;V, now shares
that nominal 230&nbsp;V figure.

Neither system is "right." The US traded efficiency for the momentum of its early
head start; Europe built later and optimized.

## Is higher voltage more dangerous?

Not in the way people imagine. In normal, everyday use, a properly insulated
230&nbsp;V appliance is no more hazardous to you than a 120&nbsp;V one — both are
designed to keep the electricity safely inside. The real danger of the voltage gap
isn't to you; it's to your **equipment**.

Plug a single-voltage 120&nbsp;V device into a 230&nbsp;V socket with only a plug
adapter and you can destroy it in an instant, sometimes with a puff of smoke. Going
the other way, a 230&nbsp;V-only device fed 120&nbsp;V usually just underperforms or
won't start — less dramatic, but still useless.

## What this actually means for you

Here's the reassuring part: you rarely have to think about any of this, because
most modern electronics handle both bands automatically.

| Band | Voltage | Example countries |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 100–127&nbsp;V | United States, Canada, Mexico, Japan |
| High | 220–240&nbsp;V | United Kingdom, Germany, France, India, Australia |

The one habit worth building is to **read the label** on each device. If it says
`100–240V`, it's dual-voltage and adjusts by itself — you only need a plug adapter
for the socket shape. If it lists a single voltage, crossing bands means you need a
converter or a dual-voltage replacement. Our guide to
