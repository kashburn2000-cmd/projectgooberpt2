## What "net 30/60/90" actually means

Net terms tell you how long you have to pay an invoice. "Net 30" means the full balance is due 30 days after the start date. "Net 60" gives you 60 days, and "net 90" gives you 90. The word "net" signals the total amount owed, with no early-payment discount baked in.

These terms are the backbone of B2B trade credit. A supplier delivers now and lets you pay later, which smooths cash flow on both sides — as long as everyone agrees on when the clock starts and how the days are counted.

## When does the clock start?

This is the first place deals go sideways. Most invoices start the count on the **invoice date** printed on the document. But some contracts use a different trigger:

- **Invoice date** — the most common default.
- **Receipt of invoice** — the day you actually receive it, which helps when mailing or approval delays are common.
- **Receipt of goods or services** — the day the work is delivered or accepted.
- **End of month (EOM)** — "net 30 EOM" counts 30 days from the last day of the invoice month.

Always confirm which trigger applies before you assume a due date. A "net 30" that runs from receipt can land days later than one that runs from the invoice date.

## Calendar days, not business days

Here's the point that trips up finance teams most: **net terms are counted in calendar days unless the contract explicitly says "business days."** Net 30 means 30 calendar days — weekends and public holidays included. It does not mean 30 working days.

This matters. Thirty business days is roughly six weeks; thirty calendar days is about four. Assume the wrong one and you either pay late or chase a customer too early. If your contract does specify working days, review the difference between [business days and calendar days](/guides/business-days-vs-calendar-days) before you count.

## A worked due-date example

Say you receive an invoice dated **Monday, June 1, 2026**, on net 30 terms.

- Start date: June 1 (the invoice date).
- Add 30 calendar days.
- Due date: **Wednesday, July 1, 2026**.

If that due date lands on a weekend or holiday, many contracts push it to the next business day — but only if the contract says so. To pin the exact date for your own terms, drop the numbers into the [business-day calculator](/).

## Early-payment discounts

You'll often see a two-part term such as **2/10 net 30**. It's a small carrot for paying early, and a strong return for buyers who can afford to.

### Reading "2/10 net 30"

It means: take a 2% discount if you pay within 10 days; otherwise the full amount is due in 30. Here's how a few common terms compare.

| Term | Discount | Discount window | Full amount due |
|------|----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 2/10 net 30 | 2% | 10 days | 30 days |
| 1/15 net 45 | 1% | 15 days | 45 days |
| Net 60 | None | — | 60 days |

Like the net period itself, these discount windows are counted in calendar days unless the contract states otherwise.
